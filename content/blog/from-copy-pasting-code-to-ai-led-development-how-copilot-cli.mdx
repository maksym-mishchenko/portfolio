If you’ve ever found yourself stuck in a cycle of copying and pasting code snippets into ChatGPT, only to explain your entire codebase over and over again, you’ll understand the frustration I used to feel. It was an endless loop: paste the code, get a fix, paste the next error, rinse, repeat. Sure, it occasionally worked, but the mental exhaustion of constantly being the "translator" between my codebase and the AI was overwhelming.

That was my starting point. Today? My AI tools don’t just help me write code—they run my engineering workflows, assist in code reviews, manage tasks across tools, and even collaborate with each other like a team of specialized engineers. The leap from basic AI chatbots to a multi-agent system has been transformational.

Here’s how I got here.

## From Copy-Paste Hell to Pair Programming with Copilot Chat

When I first started using ChatGPT, I was impressed by its ability to debug code—but it had zero context of my project. Every interaction was like starting a conversation with someone who had no idea what you were working on. If you’ve ever tried explaining a complex codebase to someone unfamiliar with it, you’ll know how exhausting that can be.

Then came **Copilot Chat**. It was a significant improvement. Integrated directly into my IDE, it could "see" my codebase and provide more relevant suggestions. I didn’t have to copy-paste code as much, but I still felt like the middleman. For example, I’d run a test in my terminal, get an error, and then manually copy the output into the chat. It was better, but still not seamless.

<Aside title="Why Context Matters">
AI models like ChatGPT work best when they have the full context of your codebase. Without it, they can only make recommendations based on the small snippets you provide, which often leads to incomplete or conflicting suggestions.
</Aside>

## The Moment Everything Clicked: Using Copilot CLI

The real game-changer was **Copilot CLI**. It’s hard to overstate how much this shifted my workflow. The CLI doesn’t just answer questions or suggest code—it *acts* like a junior developer sitting at your machine. It reads your codebase, runs commands, edits files, and even creates pull requests.

Let me share a moment that still feels a little unreal: I had a suite of tests failing, and instead of hunting through logs and debugging manually, I simply told Copilot CLI, "fix the failing tests."

## Beyond the Basics: Augmenting Copilot CLI with Skills

While Copilot CLI blew me away, I quickly realized it was only one piece of the puzzle. The real magic started when I began building **Skills**. These are like plugins or domain-specific knowledge modules that give your AI deeper expertise in particular areas.

For example, I created a TDD (Test-Driven Development) Skill. This Skill enforces the red-green-refactor cycle by automatically generating failing tests, implementing the fix, and refactoring the code. It’s like having a mentor sit next to you, constantly reminding you to follow best practices.

Here’s how you can define a basic TDD workflow Skill:

```json
{
  "name": "TDD Workflow",
  "triggers": ["write-tests", "refactor-code"],
  "actions": [
    "generate-failing-test",
    "implement-solution",
    "run-tests",
    "refactor"
  ]
}
```

Another Skill I built was a **C# Expert** that specializes in catching .NET-specific patterns. For instance, it flagged a subtle issue in my LINQ query that would have led to an N+1 query problem. This saved me hours of debugging down the line.

<Aside title="Why Skills Are Powerful">
Skills allow you to encode domain-specific knowledge into your AI workflows, making them smarter and more context-aware. Think of Skills as the "experience" your AI gains to handle specialized tasks better.
</Aside>

## Multi-Agent Systems: Building an AI Engineering Team

As I got more comfortable with Copilot CLI and Skills, I started wondering: What if my AI tools could collaborate like a real engineering team? That’s when I ventured into building a **multi-agent system**.

Here’s how it works today:

1. **Copilot CLI** acts as the senior developer. It handles coding tasks, runs tests, and creates pull requests.
2. **OpenClaw on Azure** plays the role of an engineering manager, prioritizing tasks, assigning work, and ensuring quality.
3. **OpenCode** is my autonomous coder, built to handle small, isolated tasks. I can even dispatch it directly from Telegram.

These agents don’t just work in isolation—they communicate with each other. For example, if I describe a feature on Telegram, OpenClaw breaks it into tasks, assigns the coding to OpenCode, and relies on Copilot CLI for reviews and testing.

## The Future: Standardizing Agent Communication with ACP

As I built out my multi-agent system, I hit one major roadblock: getting the agents to communicate effectively. Every connection between tools felt like a bespoke integration, full of duct tape and fragile assumptions.

That’s where **Agent Communication Protocol (ACP)** comes in. It’s a standard protocol that allows agents to discover each other’s capabilities and communicate seamlessly. In many ways, ACP feels like what HTTP was for the web—a foundation for interoperability.

## The Mindset Shift: From Instructions to Ownership

Here’s the most important lesson I’ve learned through this journey: **stop giving your AI tools instructions—give them ownership.**

When I was using ChatGPT, I’d say, "Write me a function to do X." With Copilot CLI, I started saying, "Own this feature end to end—plan it, implement it, test it, deploy it." That mindset shift was pivotal. My role is no longer to micromanage the AI but to define the high-level goals and let it figure out the details.

Today, my AI-powered engineering team isn’t just writing code—it’s running my projects, end to end. It’s a new way of thinking about software development, and I can’t wait to see where it goes next.

Imagine a world where AI agents not only run your engineering workflows but also handle product roadmaps, customer support, and even hiring. We’re not there yet, but the building blocks—like ACP—are already here.
